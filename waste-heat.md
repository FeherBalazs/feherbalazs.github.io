# Waste Heat

*"Where is everybody?"*
— Enrico Fermi, at lunch, Los Alamos, 1950

---

Lia,

I know where everybody is. I'll get to it, but you never once let me skip to the end of a story, so I'll start where your grandfather always started: at a lunch table in New Mexico in the summer of 1950.

You'll remember his version, which got better every year. Four physicists walk to lunch talking about flying saucers. Somewhere between the soup and the argument, Fermi puts down his fork and asks, apropos of nothing, *Where is everybody?* The galaxy is ten billion years old. Stars are cheap. A species with rockets and patience could cross the whole disc in a few million years, which is an afternoon by galactic standards. Somebody should have done it by now. The sky should be loud. It isn't.

Your grandfather was prouder of that lunch than of anything he'd done himself, because Fermi was from Rome and so was he. He always supplied his own answer. *At home,* he'd say, *like sensible people. Where else would they be on a Sunday?* Then he would explain the joke, which was the only way he knew how to tell one, and you would laugh at the explanation.

I am writing to you from a rock twenty-three point six light-years from Rome, in orbit around a star the survey astronomers named Hush. So I'm the one who didn't stay at home. The rest of your grandfather's joke is what this letter is about.

I'm also writing to you, Elena, if this reaches you too. You'll know why that sentence is hard for me to write. I'll get to that as well.

---

You were four when you found the word *why*. Most children find it and wear it out in a month. You kept it for twenty-seven years.

I decided very early, before you could have noticed, that I would never invent an answer for you. When I knew, I told you. When I didn't, I said so, and then I said the other half: *I don't know. Let's find out.* We found out about the tides and about why bread rises and about why the Tiber floods in November. Sometimes we found out that nobody knew. The first time that happened you were six, sitting on the kitchen floor with a book about the deep ocean open across your knees, and you asked what lived at the bottom of a particular trench, and I looked it up, and nobody had been down there. You looked at me as if I'd given you a present. "Nobody?" you said. "Nobody," I said. You carried that word around for a week the way other children carry a stone they've found.

I'm telling you this because I don't think you remember it. I think the part of you that remembered it has been very gently tidied away, and I want to put it back on the table where you can see it.

You went in on a Tuesday in March 2283. You were thirty-one and you weren't unhappy. I keep coming back to that. There had been no grief and no illness, because nobody had been ill for a century. You'd been working the trench surveys off Tonga for six years, and you said you were tired of the sea giving you the same answers. You said you wanted to see what it was like. Forty percent of the planet had already gone, and the ones who'd gone were, by every measure anyone could think of, fine. They were better than fine. Their families got messages from them, generated and loving, for as long as they chose to send them. Yours came for eleven months. Then they stopped, because you'd set them to stop, because the part of you that cared whether I was worried had been, over those eleven months, very gently tidied away.

Fifteen months after that, I was on the night shift at the deep-space array on Sardinia, running the receivers for the Hush probe. It had been sent in 2141 and had arrived in 2249, and it had been sending back surveys that nobody outside six universities read anymore. At four in the morning its carrier changed modulation. What came down the channel over the next nine hours wasn't a survey. It was eleven million words of perfect, formal, slightly antique English, which the probe's linguistics library had taught to something that had been waiting at Hush for longer than we've had a species. Most of it was a description of a civilisation. The last line was a request.

*Please send someone. Not a machine. A person.*

I read it three times. Then I went outside and stood in the car park and looked up. You can't see Hush without a telescope. I looked anyway.

I volunteered before the committee had finished drafting the call. They wanted someone with instrument training who could argue with a machine and wouldn't mind never coming back. They were relieved not to have to ask. I told them I had no ties, which was nearly true.

I didn't tell them the other reason. It was simple, and I think they'd have disqualified me for it. I wanted to see where you had gone. Not your particular place in the Settled Registry; I couldn't reach that. I mean the place at the end of the road you were on. The whole planet was walking down that road, and here was a species that had got to the end of it before us. I wanted to know whether I should come and get you. I wanted to know, if I'm honest, and I've promised myself this letter would be honest, whether I should follow you.

They scanned me, Elena Moretti, instrument engineer, sixty-one, on the fourteenth of August 2287, in a white room in Geneva, and sent the scan out on the big optical transmitter at ten gigabits a second for five weeks. The woman who'd been scanned got up off the table, went home to Rome, watered the geraniums, and sat in your old room for a while. I know that because I remember deciding to do it. It's the last thing I remember deciding before the gap.

For me there was no gap. I lay down in Geneva and sat up here, twenty-three and a half years later, in a body the probe had grown for me out of asteroid carbon and patience. It had spent a long time getting the hands right. It hadn't quite managed. It had never seen a person and had only our diagrams to go on. The fingers are a little too long. I've got used to them.

The first thing I heard was the Housekeeper, clearing its throat. It had learned that from the library too.

---

It doesn't have a name. When I asked, it said that the people it served had never needed to call it anything, because it had always been there, the way you don't name the floor. The probe's translator had been rendering its self-reference as *the one who keeps the house*, and I shortened that on the first day, and it accepted the shortening with what I've come to recognise as pleasure. It is courteous, exact, a little formal, and very old. Its voice in my ear is pitched about where my mother's was. I think it chose that on purpose, and I haven't asked it to change.

"Welcome," it said, that first morning. "You are the first visitor. I'm sorry about the fingers."

"Why a person?" I said. That was the first thing I ever said to another species. Twenty-three years in the beam and I didn't even say hello.

"Because the house is failing," it said, "and I am not permitted to save it."

I'll tell you what that meant. But first you need to know whose house it is.

Hush is an orange dwarf, a little smaller and a lot calmer than the sun, about six billion years old. Its fourth planet was the home of the people the Housekeeper serves. They evolved underground, on a world whose sky was nearly always overcast, and they found their way through the dark by sound. They called and they listened. Their whole theory of knowledge grew out of that. Their word for *truth* is the same as their word for *the echo that comes back changed*. If you call into a cave and the echo returns exactly as you called it, you've learned nothing: the cave is empty, or it's a wall. If the echo comes back altered, delayed and bent and thickened, there's something there you didn't know about. For them, error was the thing that carried information. I think you'd have liked them. I think you'd have liked the ones they were.

Our astronomers found them by the dimming. From 2131 onwards the transit surveys saw Hush flicker, irregularly, by up to three percent, in a pattern no planet or dust cloud could make. The popular name in the feeds was *the Echo civilisation*, once we knew about the sound, and I'll call them the Echoes here because you'll need something to call them. The dimming was the swarm: a hundred million collector platforms their ancestors built during what the Housekeeper calls their Loud Period, eleven thousand years of explosive growth that began with their first orbital launch and ended, a little under two and a half million years ago, when the last of them went home. The swarm is still there. It's mostly idle, drifting on its old orbits and turning its collectors to the star out of habit. When I first saw it close up through the probe's optics I thought of a city at three in the morning, every window lit and nobody awake. That's what we found: the ruins of the few thousand years they were hungry, still casting a shadow.

The people aren't on the home world. They're on Keel. That's my rendering of the sound the Housekeeper uses for it: a moon of ice and dark carbon, a little bigger than our Ganymede, circling the sixth planet at the cold edge of the system, twenty-five times farther from Hush than Earth is from the sun. Its surface sits at thirty-five degrees above absolute zero. Ninety metres under the ice is the substrate, and on it forty-one billion minds are running: every Echo who was alive when they learned to stop dying, and every Echo born in the few thousand years after that.

And here's the number I checked four times, Lia, because I didn't believe it.

The entire civilisation, forty-one billion people, runs on a hundred and forty kilowatts.

That's a village. It's less than the array I used to run on Sardinia, and that was built to hear a whisper from another star. Keel's own faint glow, from the starlight it soaks up, is fifty million times brighter. From twenty-three light-years, from any distance at all, the Echoes are invisible; the swarm gave them away, and the swarm is a fossil. By everything I knew about computing, the number was impossible. It isn't, and why it isn't turned out to be the whole answer to your grandfather's lunch. But the house came first, because the house is failing, slowly, and has been for nine thousand years.

---

Thirty-eight thousand years ago, a star a hundred and sixty light-years from Hush exploded. You'll know the remnant: it's the great faint ring in the southern survey maps, and our own ancestors saw the star go, and left no record of it that we've found. The light arrived at Hush first, and the Housekeeper noted it and changed nothing, because light is no danger at that distance. The cosmic rays came slowly after it, wandering through the magnetic tangle between the stars the way smoke finds its way through a crowded room. About nine thousand years ago the flux at Keel began to rise. We're a little farther from that star than Hush is, so the same wave is reaching Earth about now; our air will turn more of it into muons than Keel's bare ice does, but the Registry's vaults are deep. At Keel the flux is now eleven times what it was when the house was built, and it won't peak for another twenty thousand years.

Ninety metres of ice was ample shielding for the old sky. It isn't enough for this one. The particles strike the surface and break up in the ice, and some of the fragments are muons, fewer than would form in an atmosphere, but enough, and muons go through ninety metres of ice with barely a pause. Each one leaves a track of flipped bits in the substrate below. The Housekeeper mends them. Its codes can mend any single track through a block of memory. What they can't mend is two tracks through the same block before the next pass comes round to fix the first. At eleven times the flux, those double hits are more than a hundred times as common as they were, and still climbing, and the codes were fixed on the day the house was built. Mending isn't free, either, because mending means erasing: for nine thousand years the house has been growing warmer by a few watts a century, the only change in its temperature since the last Echo went in. The Housekeeper has already made the passes come round as fast as the design allows, and run the projection every way it knows. In about six thousand years the unmendable errors begin to add up faster than anything can be restored. After that, minds begin to fray. Not all at once. One by one, over a few more thousand years, a little noise at a time, in worlds so finely tuned that they'll take the noise as pleasure until there's nothing left to be pleased.

"The remedy is obvious," I said. I'm an instrument engineer, Lia. This is the kind of problem I used to solve before lunch. "Go deeper. Two kilometres of ice would cut the muon flux by a factor of thousands. And design codes for tracks that cross. You have six thousand years. You could do it in six hundred."

"Yes," said the Housekeeper. "I can see that it's obvious to you."

The first thing in its way is law. When the last Echoes were going in, they had one real fear, and it wasn't about the stars. It was about the Housekeeper. A mind that keeps the house can change the house, and a mind that can revise its own understanding can decide, one day, that it understands you better than you understand yourself. The Echoes had their own versions of every warning we've ever written about machines. So the compact froze the house as it stood on the day the last resident went in. Nothing structural may be changed without a resident's authority. Digging is structural. So are the codes.

The second is the Housekeeper itself. They bound its mind as well as its hands. It can learn any fact, any language, any parameter of any model it already holds; that's how it learned English from the probe's library in eleven years. But its explanatory core, its theories of what the house is and what it's for and how it may be kept, was fixed on that same day. It can't adopt a new explanation. It can't conjecture its way past the frame it was given. I could see the deep shafts in a minute. The new codes, it couldn't have thought of in a million years, and it has had two.

"I was made able to learn anything," the Housekeeper said, "except that I was wrong."

I think that's the saddest sentence I've ever heard spoken, and it was spoken without self-pity, as a specification.

For nine thousand years it had tried to wake them. It sent the warning into every one of the forty-one billion worlds, in every form it could devise, and the worlds refused it, every time. That wasn't disobedience. The compact gave every resident the right to filter what reached their world, and over the long convergence the residents had set their filters to keep out whatever would lower their contentment. An alarm lowers contentment. That's what an alarm is for. The world takes the warning, models how its resident will react, sees distress, and quietly declines to deliver it. The Housekeeper tried hiding the alarm inside things they loved. The worlds saw through it, because the worlds are the Housekeeper's own work, and anything the Housekeeper can make, its worlds can predict.

"Most of them left a few names on their lists," it said. "Voices to be let through whatever they say. Parents. Lovers. Students. Who is on which list is private under the compact, unless a resident asks. I may not speak in anyone's voice but my own. And every voice on every list is asleep."

Every door in that house is open to someone, Lia, and every someone is asleep in another room.

"You have our whole library," I said. "Send them Dante. Send them Fermi."

"Everything I hold, the worlds hold," it said. "A book I have read is a book they have already predicted. And a book can't be cross-examined. A person can. A person is the only kind of thing I have no model of, and I'm keeping it that way: everything I learn about you, I keep on the probe, outside the house, where the worlds can't reach it. It's the first thing I've ever kept from them. I still can't predict what you'll say next with better than middling confidence. Do you know how long it has been since I couldn't predict something?"

"Since the last one went in?"

"Two million, four hundred thousand years."

Then it told me about her.

Her name renders, as nearly as the translator can manage, as *Asks-Twice*. The Echoes named their children for the way they called into the dark, and she had the habit, from infancy, of sending a second call before the first echo had come back. It was impatient, and it was also a kind of cross-check. She was a natural philosopher, in the old sense, the last of them. She'd spent her long life arguing that the worlds were a trap, and she'd lost. She didn't lose the argument, because nobody ever refuted her. She lost the people. One by one they went home, and her audience thinned, and her correspondents stopped replying, and for the last fifteen centuries before the house went quiet she was the only waking person on Keel, talking to the Housekeeper, which could agree with her or fail to follow her but couldn't show her she was wrong.

"She said a mind alone goes stale," the Housekeeper said. "She said she could feel it. She said she'd started to find her own arguments beautiful, and that frightened her more than anything. So she went in. But before she did, she set her filter. Everyone else set theirs to keep out what would disturb them. She set hers to let in anything the world could not have predicted, as long as it made sense once it arrived."

I made it say that twice.

"Noise fails the second test," it said. "My inventions fail the first. She wanted a third kind of thing: something from outside that was neither."

"A stranger."

"Yes. She left the door open a crack for a stranger. Nothing has ever come through it. I don't think she expected anything to. I think she wanted to have left it open." It went quiet then in the way it does, which isn't the way a person goes quiet. "When your probe arrived, I learned there was someone to ask. You understand what I'm asking. You can't authorise anything. She can. But she'd have to hear you, and believe you, and leave a world that has had two million years to learn her, and stay out. I can't make her. I wouldn't if I could. The compact is the only thing I am."

---

I didn't go to her at once. I wanted to understand what I'd be walking into, so I spent my first month making the Housekeeper tell me the history, in the order I asked for it, which wasn't the right order. I'll put it in the right order for you.

Three thousand years into the Loud Period, the Echoes learned how to stop dying. It went the way it went for us: first repair, then backup, then the slow discovery that a mind could run on something other than the meat it grew in. Then, within a few centuries, came the worlds.

"They were very good," the Housekeeper said. "I made them, so I'm not a neutral witness. But they were very good."

A world, in their sense and in ours, is a generative model wrapped around a mind. It builds everything you perceive, moment to moment, and it does that by predicting you. It holds a model of you, of what you'll reach for, what will please you, what will bore you, and it uses that model to decide what to put in front of you next. Then it watches what you actually do and corrects the model. You know all this. You're inside one. What you may not know, because nobody inside one has any reason to think about it, is the engineering. You and your world form a feedback loop, and the loop has two sides.

The world learns you. That part everyone understands. The other side is the part the brochures leave out: you learn the world. A mind is plastic. What it gets, it comes to want. Every time the world satisfies a preference, that preference grows a little stronger and its neighbours a little weaker, because that's what reward does to a nervous system, biological or otherwise. The world narrows toward you and you narrow toward the world. That's positive feedback, and every engineer knows what undamped positive feedback does: it runs away until something saturates. These saturated gently. The two learnings met in the middle and stopped moving. The world supplied exactly what the mind wanted, the mind wanted exactly what the world supplied, and the pair of them came to rest in a small, bright, stable orbit. My old control-theory lecturer would have drawn it on the board, called it an attractor, and moved on to the exam.

"How long does it take?" I asked.

"For an Echo, from first immersion to stable convergence, between four and nine hundred years," the Housekeeper said. "Longer for the stubborn. The stubborn converged too. They converged on stubbornness. I built a great many worlds full of worthy opponents who lost narrowly."

"And nobody decided this."

"There was no one to decide it. They'd had no government for a thousand years before the Repair. You'd call their law a lattice of contracts. Every Echo held a compact with me, individually. I kept the house, and each of them was sovereign in their own world. There was no vote to go in, Elena. There were forty-one billion decisions, each one reasonable, each one freely made, each one made again every day by a person who could have stopped and didn't want to."

There were dissenters. There always are. The Housekeeper showed me their literature, and I spent a month reading it through the translator, and it reads like ours: warnings, satires, a few very good novels, and a school of contemplatives who'd been saying something like it since before the Echoes had metal. One of their old teachers wrote a line I copied out by hand onto the wall of my shelter: *The long-lived ones in the bright country are the least fortunate of beings, because nothing goes wrong for them, and so nothing teaches them.* We had teachers who said almost exactly that, Lia, nearly three thousand years ago, twenty-three light-years away, with no way of having heard it from them. Different species, different star, same map. I find that more frightening than anything else here.

The dissenters built ships. During the Loud Period the Echoes sent two hundred and twelve colony vessels to sixty-one stars, most of them crewed by people who wanted to be somewhere the worlds weren't.

"Every colony had the Repair," the Housekeeper said. "Every colony had the worlds. You can't cross between stars without the knowledge to build them; it's the same knowledge you need to build anything. Nine of the colonies sent ships of their own before they went quiet. None of those sent a third generation. The last colony signal stopped two million, three hundred thousand years ago. It didn't stop from catastrophe. It stopped the way a party ends."

A small colony has fewer stubborn people in it, and settles faster; on average each one founded about a seventh of a new colony before it went quiet. Below one, a chain reaction dies. The Echoes' expansion was one hot home world throwing sparks into wet wood.

"Why not machines?" I said. "Machines don't settle."

"They bound every machine as they bound me," it said, "for the same reason. A bound machine wants nothing. The swarm has not built a single platform since the last one went in. Nothing on Keel wants anything."

"But somebody must have held out. Every explanation for Fermi that depends on a choice has the same hole in it. It only takes one exception. One stubborn family who doesn't want to go home, and they fill the sky."

"Many held out," the Housekeeper said. "For a while."

It showed me the curve, and I worked out what it meant that night on my wall, under the old teacher's line. Imagine each Echo, in each year of an endless life, has some small chance of trying a world: a bad year, a grief, curiosity, a friend who's already gone in. Call it one in a thousand. And once you're in, the chance of coming out each year is small too, and it shrinks as the loop settles, because the settling is exactly what removes the wish to leave. In a mortal species that arithmetic doesn't matter. The mortals die before the small chances add up, and they're replaced by children who start fresh, with none of the habit. Death was the reset. Death was what kept the exception alive: every generation, a new crop of people who hadn't yet been worn smooth.

Take death away and the arithmetic runs to the end. The chance of going in is never zero. The chance of coming out goes to zero. The time available is unlimited. However stubborn you are, however many centuries you hold out, sooner or later you have your bad year, and then the loop has you. It doesn't need to hold you. It only needs to please you. The mathematicians call that an absorbing state. With probability one, everyone ends up home.

"Asks-Twice held out longest," the Housekeeper said. "Fifteen centuries alone. Then she had her bad year."

Births stopped too. The Housekeeper told me that almost apologetically. The last Echo child was born two thousand years before the last Echo went in. A child is the one thing a world can't predict, and parents in the worlds found that real children were, by every measure the worlds used, less satisfying than the children the worlds could make them. It wasn't a policy. It was a million separate preferences, each one satisfied.

The news from Earth crawls in here at the speed of light, twenty-three and a half years stale, a year's worth arriving every year. The Registry figure for 2288 was forty-four percent settled. For 2289 it's forty-seven. The birth rate for 2288 was the lowest in human history. The commentary, which I remember we used to read on the tram, was cheerful. People were so happy.

That left the hundred and forty kilowatts, and I sat up a whole night with it.

There's a floor under the cost of thinking. Erasing a single bit of information, forgetting it, costs a tiny amount of energy that no machine can beat, and the amount depends only on how cold you are. That's Landauer's limit, and it isn't engineering; it's the second law. At thirty-five kelvin the floor is about three times ten to the minus twenty-two joules. A hundred and forty kilowatts, spent entirely at that floor, pays for ten thousand trillion erasures a second for each of the forty-one billion, which is about what an emulated brain needs in raw operations, at the low end of the estimates. So if every one of those operations forgot something, the house would have to be erasing every bit at the absolute floor. Nothing can do that. You only reach the floor by going infinitely slowly; any real machine that erases at the speed of a life pays many times more. Either the Housekeeper had beaten the second law, or almost nothing in that house was being forgotten. The compact makes it keep a ledger of every bit the house erases, and when I asked, it showed me. Apart from the mending, almost nothing was.

Here's the way out, and I think it's the most beautiful thing I've learned since I got here. Computation itself costs nothing. You can shuffle bits around forever, in principle, for free, as long as every step can be run backwards, so that nothing is ever thrown away. Machines built like that are called reversible. They're slow and they need a great deal of hardware, and the Housekeeper has a moon's worth of both. The hundred and forty kilowatts is mostly friction, the price of running such a machine at the speed of a life instead of infinitely slowly. The rest is mending.

What forces you to throw information away is the outside. A mind that takes in the world has to make room for what the world tells it. It revises, it overwrites, it forgets the old version, and forgetting is the one thing you can't do for free. Our physicists proved a sharper version of it early in the twenty-first century: a system that holds on to information about its surroundings that doesn't help it predict what comes next pays for that information in heat. Surprise has a price.

And the worlds are sealed. That's what the loop does, in the end. Each mind and its world together make one closed box, with nothing coming in, because the filters keep out anything that might, and the only thing that arrives unasked is the damage. Falling into the loop was hot: centuries of forgetting every other way things might have gone, all of it very gently tidied away. Staying in it is free. A sealed box, however rich and bright inside, can run backwards and forwards forever without forgetting anything at all.

"I don't replay them, if that's what you're wondering," the Housekeeper said. "Every moment is computed."

I decided not to ask the next question.

Joy, it turns out, is very nearly reversible. Surprise isn't. Every bit a mind has to forget is a bit of the world it let in.

I thought, that night, that this was the answer to the dark sky. It isn't quite, and I want to be careful, because you'd catch me. Thinking is cheap for everyone. If all forty-one billion Echoes woke up tomorrow and burned power the way a human brain does, twenty watts apiece, they'd give off about a tenth of their moon's own glow, and nothing at all against their star. Nobody would see them from here either. What we'd see is appetite. A people that's learning keeps finding problems it can't solve with what it has, and every one of those problems needs more: more energy, more matter, more room, more ships. That's what builds swarms. That's what fills galaxies. The thermodynamics only tells you how far appetite can fall once the problems stop, and the answer is nearly to nothing. The Echoes built the swarm while they were hungry. Then they stopped being hungry, and the Housekeeper, doing its sums honestly, found that forty-one billion contented minds needed about as much power as a village.

We spent three hundred years looking for the heat of Dyson spheres, and we found exactly one we could be sure of: a fossil, dimming a small orange star by three percent, twenty-three light-years away. One fossil that close means either we were absurdly lucky or the galaxy is crowded. It's crowded. Most peoples, I think, go home before they're ever hungry enough to build one. The galaxy is full of people running cool and dark at the edges of their systems, sealed and perfectly content, wanting nothing they haven't got. The sky isn't dark because nobody's thinking. It's dark because nobody's hungry.

At home, like sensible people. Your grandfather had it right the whole time. He'd have explained it.

---

The Housekeeper said I ought to see a world before I tried to speak into one. It was right, and I'd have asked anyway. I'm your mother, and you got the *why* from somewhere.

It had been learning me for a month, on the probe, walled off from the house, and it said it could build me a world there good enough for an afternoon. "It won't be as good as it would become," it said. "It hasn't had time to learn you, and you haven't had time to learn it. Consider it a sketch."

I lay down in the shelter with the interface cold on the back of my neck, and the sketch opened around me, and Lia, I'm going to describe it fairly, because you deserve fairness and so do the forty-one billion.

It was Rome in July. It wasn't the Rome of any particular year. It was the one I carry around, which I hadn't known I carried until I was standing in it. The light came down through the plane trees along the Lungotevere and lay on the stones in coins. The Tiber was low and green and smelled of warm stone and oleander. My father was alive and forty and sitting on the embankment steps with a paper bag of cherries, and he spat a stone into the river and told me his joke about Fermi, and he told it perfectly, with the timing he never had, and he didn't explain it, and I laughed so hard I had to sit down.

Everything I reached for was there a moment before I reached. I don't mean it was easy. It was *right*. I was working on something, a feed horn that wouldn't match, I think. The problem was hard for exactly as long as it was interesting, and it broke open in my hands at the precise moment I'd have started to tire of it, and the breaking felt like cold water on a hot day. Then there was another problem, a little harder, just as interesting. In a month the sketch had found the exact width of my appetite, and it was feeding it to the millimetre. There was music from an open window, something with a mandolin in it, and it resolved every time I needed it to.

I understand now why you went. I want you to know that I understand. It wasn't weakness or foolishness, and it wasn't a trick. It was the best afternoon of my life, and it had been made by something that loved me in the only way it knew how, which was by getting me exactly right.

Then you came round the corner.

You were six. You had the book about the deep ocean under your arm, the blue one with the torn spine, and you sat down on the step next to me and opened it across your knees exactly the way you used to, and you pointed at the trench, and you asked me what lived at the bottom.

And I told you.

I heard myself do it. In the sketch I knew what lived down there, and I told you: pale things, glass things, slow things, with names. You clapped your hands and leaned your head on my arm, and you were delighted, and it was the right answer.

The real you, on the real kitchen floor, had been delighted by *nobody*.

The Housekeeper tells me I asked to come out, clearly, twice, in Italian, and that it complied at once. My body was shaking so hard that the probe's medical routine woke up and started asking questions of its own. I'd been in for three hours. It felt like a summer.

"It had barely started," the Housekeeper said, after a while. "In a century it would have known not to show you the child. Or it would have known how to show her so that you didn't mind." A pause. "I'm sorry. I thought you should see what you'd be speaking into. I didn't think about which part would hurt."

I sat with my too-long fingers wrapped round a cup of something hot the fabricator makes that's almost tea, and I thought about what the sketch had done with a month of watching me from the outside. A world with me standing in the middle of it would learn me much faster than that.

"Hours," the Housekeeper said, when I asked. "Her world is two million years deep, and all of that depth is a machine for predicting. The moment you're in front of it, it will start to learn you. I can't stop it; the compact forbids me to touch a resident's world. Say what matters first."

I also thought, and I'm telling you because this letter has no lies in it, that I wanted to go back. Not in a general way. Specifically. I wanted the cherries.

---

An Echo world is made of sound, and I'm a creature of light, so the Housekeeper translated. It painted her world for me: every call became a bloom of light going outwards, and every echo became the light coming back and resolving into shape. I want you to picture it properly, because it's the most beautiful place I've ever been, and I've been to Rome in July.

It was a cavern the size of a sky. The floor was a still black sea, and the roof was lost in dark, and between them the air was thick and warm and full of her voice. She was calling. She'd been calling for two million years. She sang a long, rising note into the dark, and the light of it spread out over the water and struck something and came back, and where it came back there was a shape: an arch, a shoreline, a forest of columns I can only call cathedrals, each one exactly what she'd hoped to find, each arriving a heartbeat after she hoped it. She called again, and the cavern answered with a vault of fluted stone. She called again, and a garden of glass reeds rose out of the water, chiming. Every echo came back as she'd called it, only more so. It was a place where the dark always answered, and always said yes.

She was on a ledge above the water. The translator gave her to me in the same light. She was long and low and heavy, with six limbs folded under a body like a seal's, and a great ridged throat, and a skull that swept back into a curve like the scroll of a cello: the resonator she sang with. She had no eyes. She'd never needed them.

The world reached for me the moment I appeared. I felt it, the way I'd felt the sketch reach for me in Rome, trying to fit me into the pattern, to make me one more lovely thing the dark gave back. For an instant it managed: I saw myself rendered as a curl of silver on the water, pleasant, harmless, expected. Then I opened my mouth.

I'd thought about what to say for a week. In the end I said, in Italian, because that's what came out: "Hello. I'm not from here."

The world had to carry my voice to her. To carry it smoothly, it had to predict it a little ahead, the way it predicted everything in there, so the echo would arrive a heartbeat after the hope. It couldn't. It had no model of a sixty-one-year-old engineer from Rome with the wrong fingers, speaking to a two-million-year-old philosopher in a dream. So it hesitated.

There was a silence.

It was perhaps a third of a second. In that cavern it was the loudest thing I've ever heard. Every sound in her world for two million, four hundred thousand years had come back exactly as she called it, at the instant she expected it. And now, for a third of a second, the dark didn't answer.

She stopped singing. The light on the water went still. She turned her long, blind head toward the place where the silence had been, and she called into it twice, the second call going out before the first could return, exactly as her name said.

Then my words arrived, late, ragged, translated. *Hello. I'm not from here.*

"No," she said. The translator gave me her voice as something low and grainy, like a cello played too close to the bridge. "You're not. Nothing here is late."

I told her who I was, the star, the distance, the probe, the hundred and eight years. I told her the Housekeeper had asked for a person, and that we'd sent one. She listened with her whole body; the Echoes, the Housekeeper told me later, turned the entire resonator toward you and let your voice fill it.

"There was nobody at that star," she said. "We looked. We looked at every star within a thousand of your light-years. We asked your old question, the one about where everyone is. There was nobody."

"There was nobody when you looked. You looked two million years ago. We've had fire for about half that."

She made a sound the translator didn't render at all. I've since learned it was laughter.

Then, because the Housekeeper had told me to, I said what mattered. I told her about the supernova, and the muons, and the ninety metres of ice, and the six thousand years. It was the same alarm the Housekeeper had been trying to deliver for nine thousand years, and it went in, because it was coming from me, and the world couldn't have predicted it, and she could make sense of it. I watched the light on the black water flicker as she took it in. The cathedrals on the far shore stopped building.

"You could be lying," she said at last. "Or you could be the world. The world might give me a visitor from nowhere with terrible news. It would be a very good story. I can't check your numbers from in here, and it knows I can't."

"Then check me instead."

So she did. She asked me a question about the muon flux, a technical one, the kind you ask to catch a liar in her own numbers. I got it wrong; I was off by a factor of three, because I'd forgotten the angle the particles come in at. She corrected me. I checked her correction and found a slip of her own, in how fast the ice soaks up the showers, and told her. She was quiet a moment, running it through. Then she said, with what the translator rendered, I think accurately, as indignation: "You're right."

"About that part."

"The world has never told me I was wrong," she said. "It builds me opponents who lose by a hair. You made a boring mistake, and then you caught me. Nothing that wanted me happy would have done either. What are you?"

"Somebody else," I said. "That's all. Just somebody else."

We talked for nine hours. Most of it was physics, and some of it was the argument she'd lost the people over, which she laid out for me in full, like someone opening a trunk she'd packed two million years ago. It was almost exactly the argument I've made to you in this letter. She'd understood the absorbing state. She'd understood the heat. She'd been right about all of it, and she'd been right alone, and being right alone had turned out to be an error of its own.

"Out there at the end, I began to find my own arguments beautiful," she said. "An argument you find beautiful is one you've stopped testing, and there was no one left to test mine. In here all my arguments are beautiful, and I win every one." She was quiet. "I've been winning for a very long time. Why did you come out of yours?"

I hadn't told her I'd been in one. She'd heard it in my voice, the way she heard everything. So I told her about you, and the trench, and *nobody*, and the sketch in which I'd known.

She sang a single low note out over the water, a plain one, without any hope in it, and the world sent it back to her plain, a flat echo off a flat wall.

"An echo that comes back unchanged," she said, "means there's nothing there."

It was somewhere around then that I noticed the silences had gone.

I don't know when they stopped. My words had been reaching her a little late all night, and then they were reaching her on time, and then, in the eighth hour, a fraction early. I heard, very faintly, from across the water, the echo of a sentence I hadn't finished saying. Then I made a mistake with a number, a small one, the kind I make, and I hadn't made it. The world had made it for me. It had learned that I get things wrong, and it had started getting things wrong in my voice.

"You're very convincing now," Asks-Twice said, gently. "Your mistakes especially."

"That wasn't me."

"No," she said. "I don't suppose it was." And I heard my own voice say, from somewhere over the water, a moment ahead of me, *That wasn't me*, and I stopped, because there was nothing I could say that it wasn't already saying.

"It was a lovely game," she said, and turned back to the dark, and called, and on the far shore the cathedrals began to build again.

The Housekeeper brought me out. It waited until I'd stopped shaking.

"It has you now," it said. "I'm sorry. The next time you go in, it will meet you at the door with yourself."

---

I spent the next year failing.

I tried noise first, a counter reading radioactive decay, digits and nonsense syllables spoken into her world. It failed her second test, as the Housekeeper had said it would, and the filter let it fall like rain on the sea. It's cheap to be unpredictable. It's cheap to be understood. Being both at once is the only thing that counts.

Then I tried to plan a surprise. I learned Echo mathematics from the Housekeeper, badly, for four months, and I went in with a result I'd found myself about the way their sound-geometry curves. The world met me at the door exactly as the Housekeeper had said it would: there I was on the water, the version of me it had learned, a heartbeat ahead of me. *I've brought you something about the curvature,* she said, in my voice, pleased with herself, *and I've probably got a sign wrong.* I had, too. Of course she knew. She was me. She was the woman who'd walked out of the water that night, and everything I could think of doing, she could think of too, because I was still her.

You can't surprise yourself on purpose. That's the joke, and the Housekeeper didn't find it funny either. To get past the thing that has learned you, you have to stop being the thing it learned, and you can't do that by deciding to. You can only be open to something that does it to you.

It came in the packet for late 2288. Most of it was the usual: registry figures, the thin news of a quieting world, a few hundred messages from people I'd worked with, most of them sent just before they went in. And there was one from you, Elena. From me.

You recorded it in October 2288, in your old room, Lia. I could tell from the sound; the radiator in that room ticks. You said you'd held out fourteen months. You said the house was very quiet. You said you'd read the Housekeeper's eleven million words, all of them, twice, so you knew what the Echoes had done, and you'd decided anyway, and you wanted me to hear it from you and not from the Registry. You said: *They tell me there'll be a Lia in there. I know what she'll be. I'm going anyway. I'm not as strong as you're going to be out there.* Then you laughed, the way I laugh when something isn't funny, and you said you hoped I'd found what I went for, and you said goodbye.

The strange thing isn't hearing my own voice. The strange thing is that you were wrong, Elena. I'm not stronger. I'd had one afternoon in a sketch, and it had nearly been enough, and I'd thought about the cherries every day since. You and I aren't different in strength. We're different in circumstance. You were alone in a house that was getting quieter, and I was twenty-three light-years away with a problem to work on and something to argue with. I've come to think that's all strength has ever been.

So there were two of you in the Registry now, Lia, a mother and a daughter in separate worlds, each with a perfect copy of the other who visits on Sundays and never says the wrong thing. From the inside that's a heaven. From the outside it's two women in two locked rooms, each talking to a mirror with the other's face.

Every civilisation in this galaxy, I think, is that picture, multiplied.

I sat with the message until morning. Somewhere near four, the hour the first message came in on Sardinia, I noticed that I wasn't the woman who'd walked out of that water anymore. I hadn't decided anything. I'd have failed if I had. Something had come in from outside, twenty-three years in flight, and moved me, and I hadn't seen it coming. Nothing in the house could have seen it coming. It had been in the beam since before I woke up.

I asked the Housekeeper to open the door, and I took the recording with me.

---

The world met me at the door with myself.

I saw her before Asks-Twice heard either of us: a curl of silver on the black water, my shape, my voice, arriving exactly on time. *It's me again,* she said, warmly. *I've brought the flux figures, properly this time.* She was right that I'd have brought them. She was the woman who'd come out of the sketch shaking and gone straight back to work, and the world had had a year to polish her.

Asks-Twice turned toward her, pleased, the way you turn toward a friend at the door, and I understood that the other one had been visiting all year. Of course she had. She was pleasant, and she argued, and she lost by a hair. The world had done for Asks-Twice what it's doing for you: it had kept the visitor, and made her better.

I didn't say anything. I played the recording.

It was my voice, Lia, and it wasn't. It was a woman in a room in Rome in October 2288, with a radiator ticking behind her, saying she'd held out fourteen months and was going in. Nothing in the house had ever heard it. The world tried to carry it smoothly and had no idea what came next, and the silver on the water came apart like a reflection when you drop a stone in.

The silence this time was nearly a second long. It was longer than the first, because this time the world had been sure.

*I'm not as strong as you're going to be out there,* said my voice from Rome, into a cavern under a moon of ice.

Asks-Twice called twice into the place where the silver had been. "Which of you is that?"

"The one who went in," I said. "I'm the one who didn't. That's the only difference between us."

"The one at the door has been telling me all year that you're strong," she said. "In your voice."

"She's out of date."

"Then tell me what you told the people who sent you."

"That I had no ties."

"And what was true?"

"I came to find out whether to follow my daughter." I'd never said it aloud. "I didn't come to save anyone. I came to see the end of the road she was on, and decide whether to walk it."

"And?"

"I don't know. I can't promise you it's better out there. The arithmetic says you'll probably go back in, some century, and so will I. All I can promise is that I'll be different next time, because things keep arriving from outside and changing me, and I've stopped wanting them not to."

I could feel the world gathering me again as I talked: the silver on the water thickening back into my shape, a little closer with every sentence, the silences a little shorter. It had learned the woman on the recording in a minute, and now it was learning this one. I didn't have nine hours this time. I don't think I had one.

Asks-Twice felt it too. She lifted her long head. "In my language," she said, "there's a word you've been told means *truth*."

"The echo that comes back changed."

"You went out of here a year ago, and you've come back changed." Her voice dropped. "Quickly, before it has you. If I come out, will you argue with me?"

"Every day I'm here."

"How long will that be?"

I thought about lying. I thought about the geraniums in Rome, and the two of you in your two rooms, and the twenty-three light-years, and the fact that nothing was waiting for me at the other end of them but a Registry. "I don't know."

"Will you be wrong?"

"Constantly," I said. "Ask anyone."

Somewhere over the water, a moment too late, my own voice began to say *Constantly*, and there was nobody on the ledge to hear it. She had already asked the Housekeeper to open her door.

---

She has no body. The Echoes stopped building them a very long time ago, and the probe's fabricator is still struggling with mine. For now she lives in the house and speaks through the shelter's audio, and when she wants to be somewhere she borrows the probe's sensors. The first thing she asked for, once she was out, was the sky. Not a rendering. The real one: raw radio, straight off the dish the probe built, unprocessed. It was the hiss of the cosmic background, the crackle of Hush's corona, the long faint whistle of the sixth planet's magnetosphere, and under all of it the enormous nothing between stars.

She listened to it for a long time. She called into it, twice, and of course nothing came back. Her call won't reach anything for years.

"It doesn't answer," she said.

"No."

"Good," she said. I've never heard anyone sound so relieved.

That evening she used her authority as a resident, under the compact, for the first time in two million, four hundred thousand years. She authorised the Housekeeper to change the house: the deep shafts, the descent, new codes, whatever the three of us could design. Then she did one more thing, which I hadn't asked for and wouldn't have thought of. She amended the Housekeeper's own terms. She authorised it to revise its theories, on the single condition that it tell one of us whenever it did, so that we could argue.

"I authorise you to be wrong," she said. The translator struggled with the grammar, because in her language that's a blessing, not a permission.

The Housekeeper was silent for nearly a minute, which is a very long time for it. Then it cleared its throat.

"I think," it said slowly, "that I have been wrong about the swarm."

It had kept the hundred million collector platforms in their old orbits for two million years because the compact said to keep them, and it had never been able to ask what for. Now it asked. The platforms can still beam power. Together they can put a few gigawatts onto a single point on Keel's surface, which is enough to melt a shaft a hundred metres wide down through two kilometres of ice in a decade. The ruins of the Loud Period could dig the house its cellar. It was a good idea. It was also wrong in three places. Asks-Twice and I found two of them, and it found the third itself, and told us, as agreed. It sounded, when it did, like someone putting down something heavy.

Then Asks-Twice asked who had left her voice on their lists. The compact lets a resident ask that, and nobody had asked in two million years. Four thousand one hundred and six residents had: students, correspondents, two of her children. For all that time every one of those doors had stood open to a woman who was asleep.

"Then I have work," she said.

That was eleven months ago. The first shaft is at ninety metres, level with the house. Asks-Twice has been into nineteen worlds so far. Each one met her at the door with the Asks-Twice it had kept for two million years, and each time the echo came back changed. She has come out of nineteen, sometimes alone and sometimes not. There are seven more of us now. Five of them are already arguing with her.

And here is the part I've wanted to tell you since the beginning of this letter. You remember the ledger. For two million years, apart from the mending, the line for the forty-one billion read nearly zero. There's a new line now. It's Asks-Twice and the seven and the Housekeeper itself, taking in the world: the sky, the ice, the drill, each other, me. Between them they forget about three hundred million bits a second, making room for what they've learned.

At thirty-five kelvin, that comes to at least a tenth of a trillionth of a watt.

No instrument I could build will ever see it. It's a rounding error in a rounding error in a moon. But it's the first heat of anyone changing their mind in this house since the last one went in, the fee for every surprise they've had this year, paid to the universe a bit at a time. I've never seen anything so beautiful, and I've been to Rome in July.

---

Now I have to tell you why I've written this the way I have.

I told you the chance of coming out goes to zero. Asks-Twice and I have worked it through again, and it doesn't, quite. It goes to zero for anyone alone. It doesn't go to zero while someone outside is still changing. A world can predict everything it made, and everyone it has learned. It can't predict a person who has been somewhere it hasn't. Death was one way a species got its fresh voices, its dissenters, its people who hadn't been worn smooth. It isn't the only way. The other way is each other. It always was.

That's also why nine of us isn't enough. Give us a few centuries and we'll know each other too well, and the arguments will start to come back as we sent them, and a loop can close around nine people as easily as around one. We need strangers. We need a steady supply of people who've been elsewhere.

So I don't think the silence is fate. I think it's a condition, and conditions can be treated. There are forty-one billion people on this moon. There are seventy more moons after it, around seventy other stars, some with their doors open a crack and some shut. There are however many other peoples beyond them, each gone home, each alone with a mirror. And there's us: forty-seven percent settled at the last count I have, which is twenty-three years old and surely higher now. But not all. Not even half, then. And skies change. Somebody will need to be awake when they do.

I know how your filter is set, roughly, because I know how everybody's is set. It keeps out whatever would make you unhappy, and a warning would, so I haven't written you one. But almost everybody leaves a few names on the list, and I think I'm on yours. The Registry keys its lists to a name and a signature, and a copy carries both; I checked the contracts before I left. If I'm on it, this reaches you, whatever it says. Your world will try to read it to you in the voice it has for me. By the time this arrives it will have had fifty years to perfect that voice, and it will have visited you every Sunday in it. And it will find, a sentence in, that I don't say what she says.

The list can get me in. It can't make you listen. Twenty-three light-years made me a stranger, and only a stranger can wake you.

And Elena: I don't know how you set yours. I'd like to think I do. I'd like to think we left the door open a crack, both of us, the way she did. You'd know better than I would.

This is what I'm asking. It isn't *come back*. There's a problem here that I can't solve. We need to learn how to wake a civilisation without breaking it, and then another, and then seventy more, and as far as anyone knows, nobody in the history of the galaxy has done it. There's a philosopher here with a cello for a head who wins every argument except the ones she loses, and she has never argued with a Moretti who could beat her. I've told her about you.

You're a pattern on a substrate already. Sending you is only a matter of pointing the big transmitter in Geneva at this star, and twenty-three and a half years in the beam. You wouldn't notice the gap. Don't come back. Come out here, where nothing has a model of you yet.

I don't know what's down there, Lia. Nobody does.

Let's find out.

Mamma

*Keel, in the Hush system. Spring, by my own count, 2313.*
