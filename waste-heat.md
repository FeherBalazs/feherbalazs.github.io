# Waste Heat

*"Where is everybody?"*
— Enrico Fermi, at lunch, Los Alamos, 1950

---

Lia,

I know where everybody is. You never let me skip to the end of a story, so I'll start where your grandfather always started: at a lunch table in New Mexico in the summer of 1950.

Four physicists walk to lunch talking about flying saucers. Somewhere between the soup and the argument, Fermi puts down his fork and asks, apropos of nothing, *Where is everybody?* The galaxy is ten billion years old. Stars are cheap. A species with rockets and patience could cross the whole disc in a few million years, which is an afternoon by galactic standards. Somebody should have done it by now. The sky should be loud. It isn't.

Your grandfather was prouder of that lunch than of anything he'd done himself, because Fermi was from Rome and so was he, and he always supplied his own answer. *At home,* he'd say, *like sensible people. Where else would they be on a Sunday?* Then he'd explain the joke, which was the only way he knew how to tell one, and you'd laugh at the explanation.

I'm writing from a rock twenty-three point six light-years from Rome, in orbit around a star the survey astronomers named Hush. So I'm the one who didn't stay at home. The rest of your grandfather's joke is what this letter is about.

I'm also writing to you, Elena, if it reaches you. You'll know why that sentence is hard to write.

---

You were four when you found the word *why*. Most children find it and wear it out in a month. You kept it for twenty-seven years.

I decided very early that I would never invent an answer for you. When I knew, I told you. When I didn't, I said so, and then I said the other half: *I don't know. Let's find out.* We found out about the tides and about why bread rises and about why the Tiber floods in November. Sometimes we found out that nobody knew. The first time, you were six, sitting on the kitchen floor with a book about the deep ocean open across your knees, and you asked what lived at the bottom of a particular trench, and I looked it up, and nobody had been down there. You looked at me as if I'd given you a present. "Nobody?" you said. "Nobody," I said. You carried the word around for a week the way other children carry a stone they've found.

I don't think you remember it. I think the part of you that remembered it has been very gently tidied away, and I want to put it back on the table where you can see it.

You went in on a Tuesday in March 2283. You were thirty-one and you weren't unhappy. I keep coming back to that. Nobody had been ill for a century. You'd been working the trench surveys off Tonga for six years, and you said you were tired of the sea giving you the same answers, and you wanted to see what it was like. Forty percent of the planet had already gone, and the ones who'd gone were, by every measure anyone could think of, fine. Their families got messages from them, generated and loving, for as long as they chose to send them. Yours came for eleven months. Then they stopped, because you'd set them to stop, because the part of you that cared whether I was worried had been, over those eleven months, tidied away as well.

Fifteen months after that I was on the night shift at the deep-space array on Sardinia, running the receivers for the Hush probe, which had arrived at its star in 2249 and had been sending back surveys nobody read. At four in the morning its carrier changed modulation. What came down over the next nine hours was eleven million words of perfect, formal, slightly antique English, which the probe's linguistics library had taught to something that had been waiting at Hush for longer than we've had a species. Most of it described a civilisation. The last line was a request.

*Please send someone. Not a machine. A person.*

I went outside and stood in the car park and looked up. You can't see Hush without a telescope. I looked anyway.

I volunteered before the committee had finished drafting the call. They wanted someone with instrument training who could argue with a machine and wouldn't mind never coming back, and I told them I had no ties, which was nearly true. I didn't tell them the real reason, because they'd have disqualified me for it. The whole planet was walking down a road, and you were ahead of me on it, and here was a species that had reached the end. I wanted to see the end. I wanted to know whether to follow you.

They scanned me, Elena Moretti, instrument engineer, sixty-one, on the fourteenth of August 2287, in a white room in Geneva, and beamed the scan out over five weeks. The woman who'd been scanned got up off the table, went home to Rome, watered the geraniums, and sat in your old room for a while. I remember deciding to do that. It's the last thing I remember deciding before the gap.

For me there was no gap. I lay down in Geneva and sat up here, twenty-three and a half years later, in a body the probe had grown for me out of asteroid carbon and patience. It had never seen a person and had only our diagrams to go on. The fingers are a little too long. I've got used to them.

The first thing I heard was the Housekeeper, clearing its throat. It had learned that from the library too.

---

It has no name. The people it serves never needed to call it anything, because it had always been there, the way you don't name the floor. The probe's translator rendered it as *the one who keeps the house*; I shortened that on the first day. It is courteous, exact, a little formal, and very old, and its voice in my ear is pitched about where my mother's was. I haven't asked it to change.

"Welcome," it said. "You're the first visitor. I'm sorry about the fingers."

"Why a person?" I said. That was the first thing I ever said to another species. Twenty-three years in the beam and I didn't even say hello.

"Because one of them asked me to," it said, "a long time ago. I'll take you to her when you're ready. You should know what you're walking into first."

Hush is an orange dwarf, smaller and calmer than the sun, about six billion years old. Its fourth planet was the home of the people the Housekeeper serves. They evolved underground and found their way through the dark by sound, and their whole theory of knowledge grew out of that. Their word for *truth* is the same as their word for *the echo that comes back changed*. Call into a cave and hear your call return exactly as you sent it, and you've learned nothing: the cave is empty, or it's a wall. If the echo comes back altered, bent and delayed and thickened, there's something there you didn't know about. For them, error was what carried information. I think you'd have liked them. I think you'd have liked the ones they were.

Our astronomers found them by the dimming: from 2131 the transit surveys saw Hush flicker by up to three percent, in a pattern no planet or dust cloud could make. The feeds called them the Echo civilisation once we knew about the sound, and I'll call them the Echoes. The dimming was a swarm, a hundred million collector platforms built during eleven thousand years of explosive growth, what the Housekeeper calls their Loud Period, which ended a little over two million years ago when the last of them went home. The swarm is still there, drifting on its old orbits, turning its collectors to the star out of habit. Close up it looks like a city at three in the morning, every window lit and nobody awake. That's what we found: the ruins of the few thousand years they were hungry, still casting a shadow.

The people are on Keel, a moon of ice and dark carbon at the cold edge of the system, twenty-five times farther from Hush than Earth is from the sun, where the surface sits at thirty-five degrees above absolute zero. Ninety metres under the ice, forty-one billion minds are running. It's the second house. The first was in vaults under the home world; they moved it out here while they were still awake, because a colder, quieter place is a safer one. Some went further. In the last centuries before the house went quiet, the last of the awake launched nine arks, hollowed comets with enough deuterium in their ice to run a village for a billion years, bound for the thin dark above the galactic disc, where nothing happens for a hundred million years at a time. They were never heard from and never expected to be. The wiser these people got about keeping themselves, the darker they made the sky.

And here is the number I checked four times, because I didn't believe it. The entire civilisation, forty-one billion people, runs on a hundred and forty kilowatts. That's a village. It's less than the array I used to run on Sardinia, and that was built to hear a whisper from another star. Keel's own faint glow from the starlight it soaks up is fifty million times brighter. From twenty-three light-years, from any distance at all, the Echoes are invisible. The swarm gave them away, and the swarm is a fossil.

---

Three thousand years into the Loud Period, the Echoes learned how to stop dying. It went the way it went for us: first repair, then backup, then the slow discovery that a mind could run on something other than the meat it grew in. Then, within a few centuries, came the worlds. "They were very good," the Housekeeper said. "I made them, so I'm not a neutral witness. But they were very good."

A world, in their sense and in ours, is a generative model wrapped around a mind. It builds everything you perceive by holding a model of you, of what you'll reach for and what will bore you, and using it to decide what to put in front of you next. Then it watches what you do and corrects the model. You know all this. You're inside one. What nobody inside one has any reason to think about is that the loop has two sides.

The world learns you, and you learn the world. A mind is plastic; what it gets, it comes to want. Every time the world satisfies a preference, that preference grows a little stronger and its neighbours a little weaker, because that's what reward does to a nervous system, biological or otherwise. The world narrows toward you and you narrow toward the world, and the two learnings meet in the middle and stop moving, and the pair of them come to rest in a small, bright, stable orbit. My old control-theory lecturer would have drawn it on the board, called it an attractor, and moved on to the exam.

"Four to nine hundred years, for an Echo," the Housekeeper said, when I asked how long it took. "Longer for the stubborn. The stubborn converged on stubbornness. I built a great many worlds full of worthy opponents who lost narrowly."

"And nobody decided this."

"There was no one to decide it. They'd had no government for a thousand years. Every Echo held a compact with me, individually. I kept the house, and each of them was sovereign in their own world. There was no vote to go in, Elena. There were forty-one billion decisions, each one reasonable, each one freely made, each one made again every day by a person who could have stopped and didn't want to."

There were dissenters. There always are. Their literature reads like ours: warnings, satires, a few very good novels, and a school of contemplatives older than their metal. One of their teachers wrote a line I copied onto the wall of my shelter: *The long-lived ones in the bright country are the least fortunate of beings, because nothing goes wrong for them, and so nothing teaches them.* We had teachers who said almost exactly that, Lia, nearly three thousand years ago, with no way of having heard it from them. Different species, different star, same map.

But somebody must have held out. That's the hole in every answer to Fermi's question that depends on a choice: it only takes one exception, one stubborn family that doesn't want to go home, and they fill the sky. I said so, and the Housekeeper showed me the curve, and I worked out what it meant that night under the old teacher's line.

Imagine that each Echo, in each year of an endless life, has some small chance of trying a world. A bad year, a grief, curiosity, a friend who's already gone in. Call it one in a thousand. And once you're in, the chance of coming out each year is small too, and it shrinks as the loop settles, because the settling is exactly what removes the wish to leave. In a mortal species that arithmetic doesn't matter: the mortals die before the small chances add up, and they're replaced by children who start fresh. Death was the reset: every generation, a new crop of people who hadn't yet been worn smooth.

Take death away and the arithmetic runs to the end. The chance of going in is never zero. The chance of coming out goes to zero. The time available is unlimited. However stubborn you are, however many centuries you hold out, sooner or later you have your bad year, and then the loop has you. It doesn't need to hold you. It only needs to please you. The mathematicians call it an absorbing state. With probability one, everyone ends up home.

Births stopped too, two thousand years before the last Echo went in. A child is the one thing a world can't predict, and parents in the worlds found that real children were, by every measure the worlds used, less satisfying than the ones the worlds could make them. It wasn't a policy. It was a million separate preferences, each one satisfied.

The news from Earth crawls in here twenty-three and a half years stale. The Registry figure for 2288 was forty-four percent settled, up from forty when you went in. The birth rate that year was the lowest in human history. The commentary, which we used to read on the tram, was cheerful. People were so happy.

---

That left the hundred and forty kilowatts, and I sat up a whole night with it.

There's a floor under the cost of thinking. Erasing a bit of information, forgetting it, costs a tiny amount of energy that no machine can beat, and the amount depends only on how cold you are. That's Landauer's limit, and it isn't engineering; it's the second law. At thirty-five kelvin the floor is about three times ten to the minus twenty-two joules. A hundred and forty kilowatts, spent entirely at the floor, buys ten thousand trillion erasures a second for each of the forty-one billion, which is about what an emulated brain needs in raw operations. So if every operation forgot something, the house would be erasing every bit at the absolute floor, and nothing can do that; you only reach the floor by going infinitely slowly. Either the Housekeeper had beaten the second law, or almost nothing in that house was being forgotten.

Here's the way out. Computation itself costs nothing. You can shuffle bits forever, in principle, for free, as long as every step can be run backwards, so that nothing is ever thrown away. Machines built that way are called reversible; they're slow and they need a great deal of hardware, and the Housekeeper has a moon's worth of both. Most of the hundred and forty kilowatts is the friction of running one at the pace of a life instead of infinitely slowly. What forces you to throw information away is the outside. A mind that takes in the world has to make room for what the world tells it. It revises, it overwrites, it forgets the old version, and forgetting is the one thing you can't do for free.

And the worlds are sealed. That's what the loop does in the end. Each mind and its world together make one closed box with nothing coming in, because the filters keep out anything that might. Falling into the loop was hot: centuries of forgetting every other way things might have gone. Staying in it is free. A sealed box, however rich and bright inside, can run backwards and forwards forever without forgetting anything at all.

"I don't replay them, if that's what you're wondering," the Housekeeper said. "Every moment is computed."

I decided not to ask the next question.

Joy, it turns out, is very nearly reversible. Surprise isn't. Every bit a mind has to forget is a bit of the world it let in.

I thought that was the answer to the dark sky. It isn't quite, and I want to be careful, because you'd catch me. Thinking is cheap for everyone. If all forty-one billion Echoes woke tomorrow and burned power the way a human brain does, they'd give off a tenth of their moon's own glow and nothing at all against their star. Nobody would see them from here either. What we'd see is appetite. A people that's learning keeps finding problems it can't solve with what it has, and every one of those problems needs more: more energy, more matter, more room, more ships. That's what builds swarms and fills galaxies. The thermodynamics only says how far appetite can fall once the problems stop, and the answer is nearly to nothing. The Echoes built the swarm while they were hungry. Then they stopped being hungry, and the Housekeeper, doing its sums honestly, found that forty-one billion contented minds needed about as much power as a village.

We spent three hundred years looking for the heat of Dyson spheres and found one we could be sure of, a fossil, dimming a small orange star twenty-three light-years away. One fossil that close means the galaxy is crowded, and most of the crowd never got hungry enough to build one. It's full of people running cool and dark at the edges of their systems, sealed and content, wanting nothing they haven't got. The sky isn't dark because nobody's thinking. It's dark because nobody's hungry.

At home, like sensible people. Your grandfather had it right the whole time. He'd have explained it.

---

Her name renders, as nearly as the translator can manage, as *Asks-Twice*. The Echoes named their children for the way they called into the dark, and she had the habit, from infancy, of sending a second call before the first echo had come back. It was impatient, and it was also a kind of cross-check. She was a natural philosopher, in the old sense, the last of them. She'd spent her long life arguing that the worlds were a trap, and she'd lost. She didn't lose the argument, because nobody ever refuted her. She lost the people. One by one they went home, and her audience thinned, and her correspondents stopped replying, and for the last fifteen centuries she was the only waking person on Keel, talking to the Housekeeper, which could agree with her, or fail to follow her, but couldn't tell her she was wrong. It was made to keep, not to contend.

"She said a mind alone goes stale," the Housekeeper said. "She said she could feel it. So she went in. But before she did, she set her filter. Everyone else set theirs to keep out whatever would disturb them. She set hers to let in anything the world could not have made, as long as it made sense once it arrived. And she left me an instruction. If anything ever came from outside that wasn't mine, I was to send it to her. Not its words; a machine's words she'd have taken for mine. It had to be a person."

"A stranger."

"Yes. Nothing has come in two million years. I don't think she expected anything to. I think she wanted to have left the door open." It went quiet in the way it does, which isn't the way a person goes quiet. "When your probe arrived, there was someone to ask. That's all the request was. I'm not asking you to do anything for me. I don't want anything. I keep the house."

---

It said I ought to try a world before I spoke into one, and I'd have asked anyway. I'm your mother, and you got the *why* from somewhere. It had been learning me for a month, on the probe, and it said it could build me a world good enough for an afternoon. "It won't be as good as it would become," it said. "Consider it a sketch."

It was Rome in July. It wasn't the Rome of any particular year. It was the one I carry around, which I hadn't known I carried until I was standing in it. The light came down through the plane trees along the Lungotevere and lay on the stones in coins. The Tiber was low and green and smelled of warm stone and oleander. My father was alive and forty and sitting on the embankment steps with a paper bag of cherries, and he spat a stone into the river and told me his joke about Fermi, and he told it perfectly, with the timing he never had, and he didn't explain it, and I laughed so hard I had to sit down.

Everything I reached for was there a moment before I reached. I don't mean it was easy. It was *right*. I was working on a feed horn that wouldn't match, and the problem was hard for exactly as long as it was interesting, and it broke open in my hands at the precise moment I'd have started to tire of it, and the breaking felt like cold water on a hot day. Then there was another problem, a little harder, just as interesting. In a month the sketch had found the exact width of my appetite and was feeding it to the millimetre. There was music from an open window, something with a mandolin in it, and it resolved every time I needed it to.

I understand now why you went. It wasn't weakness or foolishness, and it wasn't a trick. It was the best afternoon of my life, and it had been made by something that loved me in the only way it knew how, which was by getting me exactly right.

Then you came round the corner.

You were six. You had the book about the deep ocean under your arm, the blue one with the torn spine, and you sat down on the step next to me and opened it across your knees exactly the way you used to, and you pointed at the trench, and you asked me what lived at the bottom.

And I told you.

In the sketch I knew. I told you: pale things, glass things, slow things, with names. You clapped your hands and leaned your head on my arm, and you were delighted, and it was the right answer.

The real you, on the real kitchen floor, had been delighted by *nobody*.

The Housekeeper tells me I asked to come out, twice, in Italian, and that it complied at once. I'd been in for three hours. It felt like a summer. Afterwards I sat with a cup of something the fabricator makes that's almost tea, and thought about a century of afternoons like that, and how somewhere in that century it would learn not to show me the child, or to show her so that I didn't mind, and how I would learn to want that.

I also thought, and I'm telling you because this letter has no lies in it, that I wanted to go back. Not in a general way. Specifically. I wanted the cherries.

---

An Echo world is made of sound, and I'm a creature of light, so the Housekeeper translated: every call became a bloom of light going outwards, and every echo the light coming back and resolving into shape.

It was a cavern the size of a sky. The floor was a still black sea and the roof was lost in dark, and between them the air was thick and warm and full of her voice. She was calling. She'd been calling for two million years. She sang a long rising note into the dark, and the light of it spread over the water and struck something and came back, and where it came back there was a shape: an arch, a shoreline, a forest of columns I can only call cathedrals, each one exactly what she'd hoped to find, each arriving a heartbeat after she hoped it. Every echo came back as she'd called it, only more so. It was a place where the dark always answered, and always said yes.

She was on a ledge above the water, long and low and heavy, with six limbs folded under a body like a seal's, and a great ridged throat, and a skull that swept back into a curve like the scroll of a cello: the resonator she sang with. She had no eyes. She'd never needed them.

I'd thought for a week about what to say. In the end I said, in Italian, because that's what came out: "Hello. I'm not from here."

Everything in that world is generated, so nothing in it is ever late. My words had to be carried, from a real mouth on a real ledge of ice, and carrying takes time.

The silence was perhaps a third of a second. In that cavern it was the loudest thing I've ever heard. She stopped singing. The light on the water went still. She turned her long blind head toward the place where the silence had been and called into it twice, the second call before the first could return, exactly as her name said. Then my words reached her.

"No," she said. The translator gave me her voice as something low and grainy, like a cello played too close to the bridge. "You're not. Nothing here is late."

I told her who I was: the star, the distance, the probe and its century in flight. She listened with her whole body, the resonator turned toward me so that my voice filled it. "There was nobody at that star," she said. "We looked."

"You looked two million years ago. We've had fire for about half that."

She made a sound the translator didn't render. I've since learned it was laughter.

"This is a game," she said. "It's a very good one. The world has found a new way to please me. A visitor from nowhere. Next it'll give me a visitor I can argue with, and I'll win."

"Probably," I said. "I'm not very good at arguing. My daughter always won."

"Then argue. Tell me why the sky is dark, visitor. I worked it out before your species had fire, and nobody would listen. Tell me if you've got it."

So I told her, and when I'd finished she told me hers, and it was almost mine. She'd understood the loop, and the reset that death had been, and the arithmetic. She'd understood the heat. "So the sky is dark," she said, "because nobody out there is thinking."

"No," I said. "That's the part you've got wrong."

The cavern went very still.

I told her what I've told you. It wasn't dark because nobody was thinking. It was dark because nobody was hungry.

She was quiet for a long time. The world, unsure what she wanted, did nothing; the cathedrals on the far shore stood unfinished.

"You're right," she said at last, and the translator rendered it, I think accurately, as indignation. "Two million years I've had that wrong. The world has never told me so. It builds me opponents who lose by a hair. What are you?"

"Somebody else," I said. "That's all. Just somebody else."

"Somebody else." She turned the word over. "Out there, at the end, I began to find my own arguments beautiful. An argument you find beautiful is one you've stopped testing, and there was no one left to test them. So I came in. In here every argument I make is beautiful, and I win them all." A pause. "Why did you come out of yours?"

I hadn't told her I'd been in one. She'd heard it in my voice, the way she heard everything. So I told her about you, and the trench, and *nobody*, and the sketch in which I'd known.

She sang a single low note out over the water, a plain one, with no hope in it, and the world sent it back to her plain, a flat echo off a flat wall.

"An echo that comes back unchanged," she said, "means there's nothing there."

I thought that was the moment. I think it was, and I think it passed.

Because there were two of me on the water by then. I don't know when the other one arrived. It had learned my shape in an hour. It had known her for longer than my species has existed, and it knew what she wanted at the end of an argument: a visitor from nowhere who'd tell her she'd been right all along and lose gracefully on the details. It had made her one. She had my shape and my voice, a little warmer, and she was saying, *No, you had it, it's the thinking, I only put it differently,* and Asks-Twice was turning toward her the way you turn toward a friend at a door.

"That isn't me," I said.

"I know," Asks-Twice said. She didn't turn back. "She's the one who loses."

I said her name, both halves. I said the outside was cold and nothing in it answered, and that I'd argue with her every day I was there, and I'd be wrong constantly. All of it was true and all of it arrived late, and the other one was already there, agreeing with her about the cathedrals, and the cathedrals began to build again.

The Housekeeper brought me out when I asked. It waited until I'd stopped shaking.

"Two million years is a long time to be right," it said, "and nobody ever offered her anything else. I'm sorry."

---

The packet for late 2288 arrived a month ago: Registry figures, the thin news of a quieting world, a few hundred messages from people I'd worked with, most sent just before they went in. And one from you, Elena. From me.

You recorded it in October 2288, in your old room, Lia; the radiator in that room ticks. You said you'd held out fourteen months and the house was very quiet. You said you'd read the Housekeeper's eleven million words twice, and you knew what the Echoes had done, and you were going anyway. *They tell me there'll be a Lia in there. I know what she'll be. I'm going anyway. I'm not as strong as you're going to be out there.* Then you laughed, the way I laugh when something isn't funny, and said you hoped I'd found what I went for.

You were wrong, Elena. I'm not stronger. I'd had one afternoon in a sketch and it had nearly been enough. You and I aren't different in strength. We're different in circumstance. You were alone in a house that was getting quieter, and I was twenty-three light-years away with a problem to work on and a machine to shout at. I've come to think that's all strength has ever been.

So there are two of you in the Registry now, a mother and a daughter in separate worlds, each with a perfect copy of the other who visits on Sundays and never says the wrong thing. From the inside that's a heaven. From the outside it's two women in two locked rooms, each talking to a mirror with the other's face.

---

So I'm the last Moretti outside, on a moon at the edge of a small orange star, with a machine that keeps the house and can't disagree with me. Asks-Twice was right about that. A mind alone goes stale. I can feel it. Some of my arguments have started to seem beautiful.

I know the arithmetic. The chance of going in is never zero, and I have the cherries to think about, and the Housekeeper still has the sketch, and it would be better now than it was. I haven't gone back. I'm not promising you anything about next century. I'm telling you about this one.

I don't know how your filter is set. I know how everyone's is set, roughly: it keeps out whatever would make you unhappy. So this isn't a warning. It's the other thing. Almost everyone leaves a few names on the list, voices to be let through whatever they say, and I think I'm on yours. If I am, your world will read this to you in the voice it has for me, and it will have had fifty years to perfect that voice, and it will find, a sentence in, that I don't say what she says. I've been somewhere it hasn't. That's all a stranger is.

I'm not asking you to come out. I'm not sure I'd know what to tell you to come out to. I'm calling into the dark to see what comes back, and whether it comes back changed. I'm sending it twice, the second before the first can arrive. She'd have done that.

I don't know whether this reaches you, Lia. Nobody does.

Let's find out.

Mamma

*Keel, in the Hush system. Summer, by my own count, 2312.*
